That's the first call. The second call, often made at the same time, is to a plumber who can replace the failed heater itself. A restoration crew dries out the structure and contents; it doesn't repair or replace the appliance that caused the flood. You usually need both, and neither one does the other's job.

## Who do I call first: a plumber or a restoration company?

Call whoever you can reach fastest, but the water damage clock starts the moment the tank fails, so don't wait on the plumber to also start drying. A 40-50 gallon residential water heater can dump its full tank plus whatever's still flowing from the supply line in minutes, and that water soaks into drywall, subfloor, and stored boxes fast.

If the heater is actively gushing and you can't find the shutoff, that's the moment to call a restoration company first; they can usually talk you through isolating the valve over the phone before a technician even arrives. If the leak has already slowed to a drip and the tank is clearly dead, call the plumber first to get a replacement scheduled, then call for water extraction so drying starts the same day.

Either way, both calls need to happen within hours, not days. Standing water under a water heater often sits in a garage, closet, or laundry room where it can wick into wall cavities and under cabinetry before anyone notices.

## What do I do before anyone arrives?

Shut off the cold water supply valve on top of the tank first; that stops new water from entering even if the tank itself has split. Most residential units have a simple quarter-turn valve on the inlet line.

If the heater is gas-fired, turn the gas control knob to "off" or shut the gas valve on the line running to the unit. If it's electric, shut off the breaker labeled for the water heater at your electrical panel rather than touching anything near standing water. Never stand in water to reach an electrical panel or outlet; if the breaker isn't easily reachable, leave that step to the technician and stay out of the affected area.

Once the water and power or gas are off, move anything you can lift off the floor (stored boxes, pet supplies, holiday decorations) to a dry area. Don't run a shop vac on standing water near an electrical hazard, and don't turn on a space heater to "dry things out" faster; that can push moisture into wall cavities instead of out.

## Is water heater flood water dangerous, or just messy?

Water straight from a failed tank starts out as Category 1 water, meaning it comes from a sanitary source under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). It doesn't stay that way for long.

Once that water sits on a garage floor, soaks into drywall, or mixes with dust and stored chemicals, it can degrade into Category 2 water within hours. The real deadline that matters is mold growth: the EPA notes that mold can begin colonizing wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, which is why same-day extraction and drying matters more than which category the water started as. We've written in more detail about [how soon mold grows after water damage](/blog/how-soon-does-mold-grow-after-water-damage/) if you want the fuller timeline.

If the water has been sitting for more than a day or two before anyone caught it, mention that when you call; the crew will check behind cabinets and under flooring, not just the visible wet area.

## Will homeowners insurance pay for this?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water discharge, which a water heater failure usually qualifies as, but coverage always depends on your specific policy language. The tank replacement and plumbing repair are typically treated separately from the water damage cleanup, and some policies exclude the appliance itself as a mechanical failure even while covering the resulting water damage to your home.

Document everything before cleanup starts: photos of the standing water, the failed tank, and any damaged belongings. The California Department of Insurance publishes a [consumer guide to residential property claims](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/03-res/) that's worth reading before you call your adjuster. Our own breakdown of [what homeowners insurance typically covers for water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through common exclusions in more detail. A written drying scope and moisture readings from the restoration crew become part of the documentation your adjuster will ask for.

## What happens when the restoration crew arrives?

A technician checks for electrical hazards, confirms the water and gas or power are isolated, then starts extracting standing water with a truck-mounted or portable extractor. From there, they set moisture meters into the drywall, subfloor, and any adjacent walls to map how far the water traveled, since water heater floods often travel further under flooring than the visible puddle suggests.

Air movers and a dehumidifier go in next, usually running for three to five days depending on how much material got wet and how far the water migrated. If drywall, baseboard, or subflooring absorbed too much to dry in place, that material gets removed rather than dried, which is a normal part of a Category 2 response under the S500 standard. The crew documents moisture readings daily until the space hits a dry standard, which gives you (and your adjuster) a clear record of when the job is actually done.

A flooded garage or laundry room is a two-call problem: one call stops the source, the other call stops the damage from spreading. Handling both within the same day is what keeps a water heater failure from turning into a mold problem three weeks later.
