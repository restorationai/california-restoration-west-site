## Why Does a Mold Smell Show Up Days After the Water Is Gone?

The smell shows up because mold needs very little time to start growing once a material stays wet. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold) notes mold can begin growing on damp materials within 24 to 48 hours, well before a homeowner notices a smell.

Surface drywall and carpet often feel dry to the touch while the paper backing, the padding underneath, or the subfloor beneath it is still holding moisture. Porous materials like drywall paper, carpet padding, and particleboard trap water longer than they show it. A fan aimed at the room for a day does not reach moisture sitting inside a wall cavity or under a baseboard, which is exactly where mold tends to start.

If you want the fuller breakdown on timing, see [how soon mold grows after water damage](/blog/how-soon-does-mold-grow-after-water-damage/).

## Who Do You Call First, a Plumber, a Restoration Company, or Your Insurer?

Call a restoration company first if the leak is already stopped and you're dealing with a smell or visible damage; call a plumber first only if water is still actively running. A sudden musty odor after a repaired leak is a drying and air-quality problem, not a plumbing problem.

If water is still flowing from a pipe, fixture, or appliance, shut off the supply valve and call a plumber or, for a gas water heater, the utility if you smell gas. Once the water source is stopped, the next call is to a company that handles both structural drying and mold, since the two are connected: materials that were missed during the initial dry-out are usually what's growing mold now. Save the call to your insurance company for after mitigation has started. Documenting the damage and beginning drying promptly supports the claim rather than delaying it.

## What Happens When You Call a Restoration Company About a Mold Smell?

A technician starts with moisture mapping, using a moisture meter and often a thermal camera to find exactly which materials are still wet before touching anything else. This step matters because odor treatment applied to a room that still has a wet wall cavity behind it just masks the smell temporarily.

Once the wet areas are mapped, the plan splits into two different jobs. Structural drying, using air movers and dehumidifiers, is what happens if materials are still wet but have not yet grown visible mold. Mold remediation, following the [IICRC S520 standard](https://iicrc.org/s520/), is a different process involving containment, HEPA air scrubbing, and removal of contaminated porous material, and it starts once mold growth is confirmed rather than just suspected. Drying alone will not fix an active mold colony, and treating a smell with an odor fogger without finding the wet source just delays the real problem.



## Will Insurance Cover the Mold Remediation Too?

Most homeowners policies cover mold remediation only when it results from a sudden, covered water loss, like a burst supply line, and the claim was reported and mitigation began promptly. Many policies also cap mold coverage at a set dollar amount regardless of the water damage cause.

Gradual leaks, delayed repairs, and long-term moisture problems are typically excluded, which is part of why the timing of that first call matters. Photograph the moisture readings and any visible growth before remediation starts, and keep the invoice separating drying work from mold removal, since adjusters often review them differently. For the general rules on what a standard policy covers, see [does homeowners insurance cover water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) and the [Insurance Information Institute's overview of standard homeowners coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).
