## When do you need flood cleanup instead of calling a plumber or the city?

Call a restoration company when water has entered the home from outside, whether that's river overflow, storm surge up a low-lying street, or a storm drain backing up through a floor drain. A plumber fixes the source of an internal leak; a restoration company handles the water that's already inside the structure.

If the water is still actively rising and your street is flooded, your first call is for your own safety, not cleanup. Get to higher ground, and only return once local officials say the area is safe to enter. Flood cleanup starts once the water has stopped moving and you can safely assess the property.

If the source is a supply line, a water heater, or a washing machine hose instead of outside water, that's still a restoration call, but a different category of water. The response and the materials affected are not the same, which is part of why the first phone call should go to someone trained to tell the difference, not a general handyman.

## Why does Ventura floodwater count as Category 3, and why does that matter?

Flood water from the Ventura River, the Santa Clara River, or a street-level storm drain backup is classified as Category 3 water under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), meaning it's grossly contaminated and treated as a health hazard, not just a mess.

That classification isn't a technicality. Water that crosses a parking lot, a street, or farmland before it reaches your floor picks up motor oil, fertilizer, bacteria, and often sewage from overwhelmed storm drains. Low-lying stretches near the Westside and the areas close to the Fairgrounds, where the Ventura River empties toward the coast, see this kind of street-level flooding during heavy winter storms.

Because it's Category 3, porous materials that absorbed the water, carpet pad, baseboards, drywall below the waterline, insulation, generally get removed rather than dried in place. Workers wear PPE, and surfaces that are kept get treated with antimicrobial before drying begins. This is also why mold risk after a flood is higher than after a clean-water leak: [mold can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours](/blog/how-soon-does-mold-grow-after-water-damage/) on wet material, and contaminated water accelerates the materials that have to come out anyway.

## What happens when you call for flood cleanup?

The first call is for triage: where the water came from, how high it got, and whether it's still moving. Have your address, a rough idea of which rooms were affected, and your insurance policy number ready if you have it.

On arrival, a technician assesses the category and class of water, documents the damage with photos and moisture readings (this documentation protects your insurance claim later), and starts extraction with truck-mounted or portable pumps. Containment or antimicrobial treatment follows if the water is Category 3, then drying equipment goes in once contaminated materials are removed.

You'll get a written scope before structural repair work begins. Every flood is different in how far the water traveled and what it touched, so an accurate scope depends on that on-site assessment, not a phone estimate.

## Does homeowners insurance cover flood cleanup in Ventura?

Usually not, and this is the single most common surprise after a flood. A standard homeowners policy typically covers sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst pipe, but excludes flood: surface water, river overflow, and storm surge are specifically carved out ([Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/facts-about-flood-insurance)).

Flood coverage comes from a separate policy, usually through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) or a private flood carrier, and it has to be purchased before the loss. If your home sits in a FEMA-mapped flood zone near the river corridors, check whether you already carry an NFIP policy; if you don't know, your agent or the [California Department of Insurance's residential claims guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/03-res/) can help you confirm what you're working with before cleanup costs start piling up.

If the water actually came from a backed-up sewer line or a failed sump pump rather than a river or storm surge, that's a different issue covered only if you carry a specific water backup endorsement, not automatically included in either a homeowners or flood policy. Either way, document everything with photos before cleanup starts and keep every receipt.
