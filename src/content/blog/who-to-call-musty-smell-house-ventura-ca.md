## What does a musty smell in a house usually mean?

A musty smell means mold or mildew is actively growing somewhere nearby, even if you can't see it. Mold releases microbial volatile organic compounds, mVOCs, as it digests organic material like drywall paper, wood, or carpet backing. That's the earthy, damp-basement smell you're noticing. It's not dust, and it's not "just an old house smell."

The [EPA notes mold can start growing within 24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq) on a wet or damp material, so by the time you can smell it consistently, there's usually been enough moisture around long enough for colonies to establish. Smell alone won't tell you how big the problem is or exactly where it's hiding, which is why a walk-through inspection with a moisture meter matters more than a plug-in air freshener.

## Is it hidden mold, or just damp coastal air?

It's worth ruling out hidden mold before assuming the smell is just marine-layer humidity, because the two look identical from the couch. Damp air alone usually fades once you run the HVAC fan or open windows for a day. A smell tied to actual growth does not fade. It gets worse in a closed room and often concentrates near one spot: a closet against an exterior wall, behind the washing machine, under carpet near a sliding door, or inside a bathroom exhaust duct that never got cleaned.

Our [guide to the signs of hidden mold](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/) covers the visual and tactile clues that go along with the smell, discoloration, a soft or bubbled spot in drywall, peeling paint near a baseboard. If you're seeing any of those along with the odor, that's not a humidity problem anymore.

## When is it actually a leak, not just damp air?

A musty smell paired with a visible stain, warped flooring, or peeling paint usually points to an active or recent leak, not ambient humidity. Winter storms that drive rain sideways into window trim, a slow pinhole leak in copper supply line, or a condensation line that's been dripping into a wall cavity for months can all produce a smell long before any obvious water shows up on the surface.

If you can trace the smell to one specific wall, ceiling corner, or window, you're probably dealing with a water source, not just regional humidity. That changes the inspection: a technician needs to find and stop the source before drying or remediation does any good. Our post on [how soon mold grows after water damage](/blog/how-soon-does-mold-grow-after-water-damage/) walks through that timeline in more detail.

## Who do you call first: a plumber or a mold company?

Call a plumber first only if you can see or hear active water, a dripping line, a running toilet, a wet spot that's spreading. Stopping the source always comes before anything else. If there's no active leak and it's just the smell, a mold inspection is the right first call, since that's what identifies whether there's a hidden moisture source at all.

A IICRC-certified restoration company can usually do both ends of this: confirm whether there's an active leak with a moisture meter, and assess whether mold growth has already started. That saves you from calling two different companies for what might be one problem.

## What happens during a musty-smell inspection?

A real inspection starts with a moisture meter scan of the walls, flooring, and any area near plumbing, not a quick sniff test. The technician checks baseboards, window sills, closets, and spots near supply lines for elevated moisture readings. They'll also check the HVAC system and any exhaust ducts, since a disconnected bathroom fan duct dumping humid air into an attic is a surprisingly common cause.

If a moisture source is found, you get a written scope of what needs to dry out or come out, following the [ANSI/IICRC S520 mold remediation standard](https://iicrc.org/s520/). If the readings come back normal and the smell traces to something like a musty HVAC filter or an un-vented closet, that's a much smaller fix, and you'll know that before paying for remediation you don't need.

## Why air fresheners and odor sprays don't fix it

Air fresheners mask mVOCs; they don't remove the moisture producing them. Spraying scent into a room with an active mold colony behind the wall just means the smell comes back within a day or two, and often gets worse, because the growth keeps spreading while nobody's looking at it. The same goes for bleach wiped across a visible spot: it can lighten surface staining but does nothing for what's growing inside a wall cavity or under flooring.

If the smell has been there for more than a week despite cleaning and ventilating, that's the signal to stop treating the symptom and get the source checked.
